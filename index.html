The Swarm

You just saw thousands of bees appear out of nowhere and settle in a mass on a nearby tree. It looks freakish, but this is actually one of the coolest and most carefully orchestrated events in the world of bees.

Section 01

What is a swarm?

A honeybee swarm is how a colony reproduces. Normally a swarm is not a sign of danger, disease, or disturbance, but swarming can sometimes occur in unhealthy colonies. A healthy hive that is ready will reproduce by swarming, creating two different colonies where there was one before.

When a colony grows large enough, roughly half to 3 quarters of the bees leave with the old queen to start over somewhere new. They leave behind developing queen cells and some workers to protect and nurture them, as well as a supply of honey.

The group that left, generally called the prime swarm, typically numbers between 8,000 and 15,000 bees, but this depends on the size of the original colony. They leave in a giant sparse cloud of bees, then settle into a tight cluster on a branch, fence post, or really any convenient surface nearby. Then, they wait while a small group of scouts searches for a new home.

Swarming bees are about as docile as they ever get. Having just gorged on honey before departure, and with no hive or brood to defend, they have little reason to sting.

- University of Florida
Section 02

Why swarming happens

There are several conditions that could push a colony toward swarming. In most cases, more than one factor is at work at once.

Overcrowding

In the spring and summer, the comb tends to fill up pretty fast since there is an abundance of nectar. If the queen has nowhere to lay eggs and workers have nowhere to store their food, the colony will begin to prepare for a split.

Reproductive drive

AFter a successful winter buildup, a healthy colony follows a deep biological impulse to reproduce at the species level. This instinct is strongest during the warm temperatures and long days of early summer.

Weakening queen signal

As the population of the hive grows during the spring, the queen's pheromones (which normally suppress the bees'urge to swarm) become too diluted across all of the workers. The lack of the chemical tells the workers to begin building queen cells.

External Factors

There are a lot of other factors that can contribute to a hive swarming, like too many mites, disease outbreaks in the colony, or pest infestations like wax moths. In these cases, a colony may swarm as a survival mechanism to escape the bad environment.

Signals vs. Cues

In bee biology, a signal is a communication evolved specifically to change the behavior of others (like queen pheromones or the waggle dance). A cue is simply information a bee picks up from its environment that wasn't intentionally sent, like if the hive is crowded or a drop in temperature.

Section 03

Inside the hive before the swarm

Swarming isn't generally a sudden surprise. The colony spends weeks preparing, and a beekeeper who knows the signs should spot them before they leave.

Queen cells appear

Workers begin constructing longer cells along the lower edges of frames. These are cells for raising new queens. Their presence is an early sign that swarming is being prepared.

The current queen slims down

Workers stop feeding the old queen as much, causing her to lose weight. If the queen is lighter, she can fly better, and she will need to fly when the swarm departs.

Scout bees begin searching

A few hundred (depending on the size of the colony) foragers start leaving the hive to investigate potential nest sites. They will inspect hollow trees, wall cavities, and other enclosed spaces, measuring volume and entrance size.

Bees gorge on honey

Right before departure, swarm bees fill their honey stomachs with about three days worth of energy reserves. This should last them through the transition to a new home.

The buzz run signals departure

Scout bees scuttle rapidly over the combs, vibrating their wings and bumping into other bees. This "buzz run" is the signal to launch. The swarm will begin to fly within minutes.

Section 04

The swarm in motion

When the swarm launches, it forms a crazy airborne cloud. For a few minutes, the bees swirl in what looks like totally random directions. As a whole, though, the swarm has a specific direction in mind.

Only maybe 5% of the bees in flight are scouts, and they are the only ones who know where they are headed. Research showed that scouts guide the swarm by flying through it at a slightly faster speed than the rest, pointed toward the target site. The uninformed bees follow the average direction of those around them, so on average, the entire swarm moves the right way.

The swarm almost always lands nearby first, usually within 100 meters of the original hive. This temporary cluster (called a bivouac) is not a permanent home, only a staging point while the scouts finish deciding on a new location.

The bivouac can hold in place for a few hours or up to several days. During that time, the bees regulate their temperature, keeping the cluster core at around 35 degrees Celsius to keep the queen healthy.

Section 05

Finding a new home

The way a swarm chooses its new home is one of the more fascinating decision-making processes in nature. It's also one of my favorite parts about bees. The researcher Thomas Seeley spent decades studying it, and described it in his book Piping Hot Bees and Boisterous Buzz-Runners.

Between 200 and 300 scout bees fan out from the cluster, searching a large area (up to 30 square miles) of surrounding land. Each scout checks out potential spots independently, looking for a few things: a volume close to 40 liters, a small entrance, a south-facing opening, and a location several feet off the ground.

When a scout finds a promising site, she will return to the cluster and performs a waggle dance. The duration of the dance and its intensity reflect how good they think the site is. Other scouts watch these dances, visit the site themselves, and come back to dance in support if they agree. Scouts who found weaker sites dance less and less over time, eventually stopping altogether.

When enough scouts are all dancing for the same site, they reach a quorum. Those scouts start to produce a "piping" signal, where they press their thorax against other bees and vibrate. This, witha bit more buzz-running, is a signal to launch. The whole swarm lifts off and flies directly to the new home.

Seeley's experiments showed that swarms chose the best available site out of several options in four out of five trials.

Section 06

What should you do?

The answer depends on who you are.

A swarm in your yard is not an emergency. Swarming bees are calm and focused on finding shelter. Here is what to do.

  • 1
    Leave them alone

    Keep people and pets away from the cluster. Do not disturb the bees or spray them or anything. They will leave you alone if you leave them alone.

  • 2
    Wait and watch

    Most swarms move on within 1 or 2 days once the scouts have reached a decision. If the cluster is not in your way, the easiest option is simply to wait.

  • 3
    Call a local beekeeper if needed

    If the swarm is in a bad location or you need it removed sooner, you should contact a local beekeeper. Many beekeepers will collect swarms for free since a swarm is a free colony of bees.

Allowing a colony to swarm is a way to respect its natural reproductive cycle. Here is how to facilitate a successful split and maybe capture the new colony.

  • 1
    Check on your colonies

    If you keep bees, you should go look in the hive rather than read this website! Keep an eye out for their development as a signal to prepare your equipment.

  • 2
    Set up swarm traps (bait hives)

    Position bait hives around your property. A 40-liter box placed 10 or 15 feet high is ideal, but lower to the ground is ok as well. If you can, try to use old comb and a few drops of lemongrass oil to mimic the scent of the scout's pheromones, making the trap much more enticing to scouts.

  • 3
    Hive the new colony

    If the swarm lands in a trap or on a low branch, carefully move them into their permanent hive. If using a trap, wait until dark when all foragers are inside, then seal the entrance and relocate them to their new stand.

  • 4
    Support the parent colony

    The original hive is now seperate but queenless. Make sure they have enough honey stores to last until the new virgin queen completes her mating flights and begins laying eggs.
